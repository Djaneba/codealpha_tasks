    The universal red-flag checklist

    Sender

    Display name looks right but the address doesn't; lookalike domain; reply-to differs from sender; unexpected sender.

    Tone

    Urgency, threats, too-good-to-be-true offers, unusual secrecy ("don't tell anyone"), generic greeting.

    Request

    Log in, confirm details, pay, buy gift cards, change bank details, enable macros, scan a QR code.

    Links & files

    Hover text ≠ shown text, shortened links, double extensions (.pdf.exe), .html/.zip/.iso attachments, macro documents.

    Modern phishing can look perfect. AI tools now write flawless, personalised emails and attackers send them from real, compromised accounts. Spelling mistakes are a bonus clue, not a requirement. Judge the request, not just the look.

    Module 4 · Recognising fake websites

    Read a URL like a security analyst

    The only part that tells you who owns a site is the registered domain: the name immediately to the left of the top-level domain (.com, .ma, .org...), just before the first single /.

    https://login.account.microsoft.com/en-us/security
    https://microsoft.com.account-verify-login.net/en-us/security
    ■ real domain (owner)■ subdomain (anyone can choose it)■ attacker's domain

    Both links start with "microsoft.com", but the second belongs to verify-login.net. Read the domain from right to left, starting at the first single slash.

    Common fake-website tricks

    Typosquatting

    paypa1.com, arnazon.com (rn looks like m), gooogle.com.

    Homograph (IDN)

    Cyrillic "а" instead of Latin "a": аpple.com looks identical. The browser may show xn--pple-43d.com.

    Subdomain abuse

    yourbank.com.secure-check.io: the real owner is secure-check.io.

    The padlock myth

    HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest. Most phishing sites today use HTTPS.

    Cloned login pages

    Pixel-perfect copies of Microsoft 365, Google or bank logins. Your password manager won't autofill on them: a great warning sign.

    Shortened & QR links

    bit.ly/... or QR codes hide the destination. Preview before opening; never log in from a QR code.

    Safest habit: never log in from a link in a message. Type the address yourself or use your bookmark or the official app.

    Module 5 · Social engineering

    Attackers hack people, not just computers

    Social engineering exploits normal human reflexes: helpfulness, respect for authority, fear and curiosity. Recognising the emotion they trigger is your best defence.

    Other social engineering techniques

    • Pretexting: inventing a believable story ("I'm from IT, we're migrating your mailbox") to obtain information.
    • Baiting: leaving infected USB keys labelled "Salaries 2026" in the car park, or offering free downloads.
    • Tailgating / piggybacking: following an employee through a secure door "because my badge doesn't work".
    • Quid pro quo: "free tech support" calls that ask you to install remote-access software.
    • MFA fatigue: spamming push notifications until the victim taps "Approve" to make them stop.
    • Deepfakes: AI-cloned voices or video of an executive asking for an urgent transfer.
    The golden rule: strong emotion + urgency + an unusual request = STOP. Verify through a second, trusted channel (call back on a number you already know).

    Module 7 · Best practices

    Your anti-phishing toolkit

    Tick each habit you commit to adopting.

    I think I clicked. What now?

    Speed matters more than embarrassment. Reporting quickly is never a mistake; hiding it is.

    1. Disconnect the device from the network (Wi-Fi off / cable out) if you opened a file or installed something.
    2. Change your password immediately, from a different, trusted device, and anywhere you reused it.
    3. Report to IT/security right away (Report Phish button or helpdesk). Mention what you clicked and when.
    4. Call your bank if you entered payment details, so they can block the card.
    5. Revoke sessions and check MFA settings, mail forwarding rules and recent activity on the account.
    6. Stay alert for follow-up attacks: attackers often call "from the bank" afterwards.

    For organisations

    Email authentication

    Enforce SPF, DKIM and DMARC (p=reject) so attackers can't spoof your domain.

    Phishing-resistant MFA

    FIDO2 security keys / passkeys can't be phished like SMS codes or push approvals.

    Process controls

    Dual approval and call-back verification for payments and bank-detail changes.

    Training & simulation

    Regular awareness sessions, simulated phishing campaigns and an easy one-click report button.
